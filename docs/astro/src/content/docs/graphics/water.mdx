---
title: Water
description: A WaterBody component that builds an ocean or a lake, lifts its geometry with waves the CPU can read back, follows its own up when tilted, and the Water shader that draws it.
---

Water has two halves. The **Water shader** (`builtin:water`) is the look: refraction, depth, reflection, foam. The **`WaterBody` component** is the
body: it builds the geometry, lifts it with waves, and answers questions about its surface from the code, so what a script reads is what is drawn.
This chapter is both, and what is not there yet.

Water is an optional module: a package takes it only when a scene authors a `WaterBody` (or a script imports `esengine/water`), so a 2D game
carries none of it.

## The component

Add a `WaterBody` to an entity. The entity places and tilts the water; its child is the mesh, made when the scene runs and rebuilt when the shape
changes.

| Field | What it does |
|---|---|
| `form` | **Ocean**: without an edge, follows the camera. **Lake**: a rectangle of `sizeX` by `sizeZ` centred on the entity, or the outline in `boundary`. **River**: follows the `Spline` on the same entity. |
| `flowSpeed` | A river's current, in world units a second where it is as wide as its widest. |
| `boundary` | A lake's outline: three points or more round it on its own xz plane. Empty is the rectangle. |
| `shoreWidth` | How far in from the edge, and how deep, the waves take to come to full height. 0 leaves them whole to the edge. |
| `material` | A material on the Water shader. Empty uses the stock water. |
| `cellSize` | The cell size of the finest ring of geometry. |
| `cells`, `rings` | An ocean's rings: cells along a ring's side, and how many rings. |
| `waveHeight`, `waveLength`, `waveSpeed`, `waveDirection` | The waves (below). |

The body writes its waves, its cell size and its position in space into its own copy of the material every frame, so editing a field moves the
water at once and several bodies can share one material asset.

## Waves

Four peaked sines (`exp(sin(theta) - 1)`: a crest narrower than its trough) a few directions apart, each with a length, a height and a speed
that are shares of the four numbers on the component. They **move the geometry**: the shader lifts each vertex by the wave height, and the
fragment takes the slope per pixel from the same formula, so the lit surface is the one that is drawn and the silhouette against the sky rises
and falls with the waves.

The waves are measured in the body's own space, along its surface and up its own up, so a tilted body is tilted water. Parameters are in world
units: a scene in metres wants the defaults, one in centimetres wants the lengths scaled.

## Geometry

A **lake** is a grid of `cellSize` cells. An **ocean** is rings of the same number of cells, each twice as coarse as the one inside it with a hole
where that one sits, so it reaches far and spends its triangles near. It follows the camera on a lattice all its rings line up on, and the
material's origin moves by as much, so the waves stay put in the world while the mesh moves under them.

A coarse ring cannot draw a short wave: below three cells to a wavelength it would alias, so its vertices fade out the waves shorter than six of
its cells (the shading still shows them, per pixel). Where two rings meet, the coarse one has half the vertices along the edge, and the surfaces
can part by what the waves do between them; keep `waveLength` at least six of the coarsest visible ring's cells and there is no seam to see.

## Lakes and shores

A lake is cut to its outline, and the waves know where its edge is and how deep the water is. The body bakes a small field over the box of its
outline: the signed distance to the edge, and the depth of the water over the ground under it. From the two comes the share of the waves a point
keeps (none at the edge or where the water is not deep, all from `shoreWidth` in), written into the mesh's vertices so the geometry calms toward
the shore, and into a texture so the pixels do too, the edge is cut where the outline is (not at the grid's cells), and the shore foams along it.

With a `Terrain` in the scene and the lake level, the ground under it is the terrain: the water fills the low ground and meets the hills where they
cross it (the depth test does that), and the waves die out as it shallows. The field is rebuilt when the terrain changes or the lake moves; a tilted
lake, or one over no terrain, is as deep as it likes. The outline should not cross itself.

## Rivers

A river is a ribbon laid along the `Spline` on its own entity ([Splines](#splines)): a row of vertices across the water every `cellSize` along the curve,
as wide as the curve says there, at the height of the curve under it, so a river runs downhill. Put a `Spline` and a `WaterBody` with `form` River on one
entity and the river is built when the scene runs.

- **The current.** `flowSpeed` is how fast the water runs where the river is as wide as its widest; a narrower reach runs faster and a wider one slower,
  within a quarter and double of it, as the same water through a different width does. The direction is the curve's.
- **The surface rides it.** The shader carries the waves and ripples downstream: two copies of the surface are moved along the current a cycle at a time, each
  fading in as the other fades out, so the moment one is put back at its start is hidden by the other, and the surface does not stretch where the river bends.
- **Flat geometry.** A river's geometry is not lifted by the waves (its surface is drawn flat and moved only in the shading), so a river has no waves to read
  back: `Waters.sample` gives its curve's height, a normal of up, and the current in `flow` (world units a second, along the river; zero elsewhere).
- **Banks.** The edge is the ribbon's own, so it is as crisp as the geometry. Foam where it meets the ground comes from the depth of the scene, as at any shore.

## Floating

Put a `Floating` component on an entity with a `RigidBody3D` and a collider and the water holds it up and carries it along. It works through forces on the
rigid body, from asking the water's surface ([Asking the surface](#asking-the-surface)) where it is under a few points of the body, so it needs no renderer:
a server, a test or a build that draws nothing floats things the same.

| Field | What it does |
|---|---|
| `buoyancy` | The water's density against the body's. 1 holds it up exactly when it is wholly under; 2 floats it half under. |
| `drag` | How hard the water resists the body moving through it, per second. It brings a body to rest in still water and carries it with a river's current. |
| `radius` | How deep, in world units, a point of the body has to be under the surface to be wholly wet. |
| `points` | Points of the body, in its own space and world units, the water is asked about. Spread them (the corners of a hull) and the waves tip it. Empty is the one at its origin. |
| `wake` | How much of a ripple the body makes in water that keeps ripples ([Ripples](#ripples)). 0 makes none. |

At each point the share of it under water (0 at the surface, 1 from `radius` down) gives a buoyant force against gravity of `mass * g * buoyancy * share / points`,
applied at the point, and a drag against the point's velocity relative to the water (the body's own and what its turning adds there, less the current). The forces are
applied every fixed step before the world steps. A body with `buoyancy` 2 and one point settles with its centre `radius / 2` under the surface.

It needs the 3D physics module, and a build of it with the force functions (`_physics3d_addForce`, `_physics3d_getBodyDynamics`); one without them warns once and
floats nothing. Floating is one rigid body in one fluid: there is no added mass, no lift from the shape of a hull, and a body that is wholly under is not pushed
toward anything but by the current.

## Ripples

A body of water keeps what disturbs it when `ripples` is above 0. The water holds a square window of its surface (`rippleSize` world units a side, `ripples` cells along it)
as a grid of heights and steps it with the wave equation: every cell is pulled toward its neighbours and carries the velocity it had, so a bump put in spreads as a ring
at `rippleSpeed`, loses `rippleDamping` of its height a second, and is gone. Beside the heights the grid keeps **foam**: a disturbance feeds it where it happened and it fades
at `foamFade` a second, so a splash leaves white water that lingers and thins and does not blink out with the ring.

- **Who disturbs.** A script calls `Waters.disturb(x, y, z, radius, strength)` (the point goes to the body that holds it; `strength` is a height in world units, negative is a dip),
  and a `Floating` body does it itself: what goes down into the water, comes up out of it or moves along its surface pushes the water by its speed (`wake` multiplies it).
- **What it changes.** The shader reads the grid as a texture: the slope of the ripples bends the normal, which is what the light, the reflection and the refraction see, and the foam
  whitens the surface. The geometry is not lifted by it. The surface the code reads does carry them: `Waters.sample` adds the ripple's height to the surface, and `Waters.rippleAt(x, y, z)`
  gives it alone, so a floating ball is lifted by the ring that passes under it.
- **Where.** The window follows the active camera, in whole cells, so a lake or an ocean of any size has ripples where they are seen for the price of one grid. What the window leaves behind is
  forgotten, and what it comes to is still water. A wave reaching its edge goes out and is not reflected. The grid is stepped on the CPU, in as many substeps as keep a wave from crossing more than
  half a cell, and uploaded as an RGBA8 texture (red the height, green the foam): the same on every backend.

The grid can hold as much height as `max(2 * waveHeight, rippleSize / 50)`: a disturbance that goes on (a body moving) saturates there and does not pile up. A cell is `rippleSize / ripples`
world units, so a ripple narrower than a cell or two is not drawn. Cost is `ripples` squared per substep and one texture upload a frame: 128 is sixteen thousand cells.

## Under the surface

When the active camera is under a body of water the picture is the water's. The water module reports where the camera is each frame (from [`Waters.sample`](#asking-the-surface))
and the post-process volume system adds an **Underwater** effect for as long as it is, the way a weather adds its rain:

- **Fog by water crossed.** The light of each pixel has crossed the distance from the eye to what it came from (the sky, which has no surface, the longest the water is seen
  through, `u_range`), and the water takes it out colour by colour, red first, by the same `u_absorption` and `u_density` the surface's material draws with, and gives back
  its own colour. A white wall under water is blue-green, and a far one more than a near one.
- **Dimmer with depth.** The colour the water gives back dims with how far down the camera is (`u_lightFalloff`), as the light that reaches it comes from above.
- **A waterline across the lens.** Each pixel is asked whether its place on the camera's near plane is below the surface at the camera, so a camera with its lens half in the water has
  a dry upper half and a wet lower half with a soft line between them (`u_band`, a twelfth of the near plane), and crossing the surface is the line sliding up the lens and not a cut.
  The effect is on from a couple of near planes above the surface down.
- **The sun through the waves.** What the camera sees is lit by the web of bright threads the waves focus the sun into: the same pattern the surface draws from above (`u_causticStrength`,
  `u_causticScale`, `u_causticSpeed` on the Water material, which the effect is given), on what faces the sun and is not in its shadow, less the further the sun's light went through
  the water to reach it. It follows the first directional light, and its shadow map where there is one.
- **Light shafts.** The water along each ray scatters the sun toward the camera: the pattern is marched along the ray in 24 steps, each lit where the shadow map says the sun reaches it
  and as much as the water above it lets through, which is what makes shafts (`u_shaftStrength`, `u_shaftRange`). It costs 24 taps a pixel in the water.
- **Yours if you wrote it.** A scene that authors an `underwater` effect in a volume keeps its numbers; only the camera's depth and how wholly it is under are filled in.

The surface itself, seen from below, is turned over and light goes the other way through it: from out of the water rays past the critical angle (about 49 degrees) are reflected
whole, so the water above is a window of sky overhead in a mirror, and the Fresnel term follows. The window's edge is not drawn as a circle on its own: it is where the
refraction runs out. What the mirror shows is the world below it: the reflected ray is followed through the picture of the scene the frame copied (20 steps, growing), and the colour of
what it meets is what the water reflects, so a box on the bottom appears in the surface above it. Where the ray meets nothing on screen the mirror is the water's own dark colour.

Seen from above, the same caustics light what is under the water through the surface: the Water shader finds where the refracted ray ends, where the sun's ray to that place crossed the
surface, and lights it by the pattern there (and by the shadow map), so the web bends with the ripples that refract it.

## Splines

`Spline` is data and nothing draws it: `points` (each an `x`, `y`, `z` in the entity's own space and a `width`) and `closed`. A river reads the one on its
entity; anything else that follows a path can read it with `SplineCurve`:

```ts
const curve = new SplineCurve(spline.points, spline.closed);
curve.position(s);   // the point a distance s along it
curve.tangent(s);    // the direction of travel there
curve.width(s);      // the width there, eased from one point's to the next's
curve.nearest(x, z); // the nearest place on it in the xz plane: s, how far, which side
```

It is a centripetal Catmull-Rom curve (it passes through every point, and does not loop or cusp where they are close at one end and far at the other)
measured by distance along it, so a place on it is `s` units from its start and not a parameter that runs fast where the points are close. Past either
end of an open curve it is the end; a closed curve goes round. Splines are an optional module (`esengine/spline`).

## Asking the surface

`Waters.sample(x, y, z)` (the `WatersRes` resource) answers for the body that holds a point:

- `depth`: how far the point is above the surface along the body's up (negative under it), in world units;
- `normal`: the surface's normal there, in world space;
- `surface`: the world position on the surface straight above or below the point;
- `inside`: whether the point is over the body's extent: inside a lake's outline or a river's banks (an ocean has none, so it is always);
- `flow`: how the water moves there, in world units a second (a river's current; still water is zero).

It reads the same formula the shader draws, at the time of the frame. The body's position, rotation and uniform scale are taken into account.
`Waters.heightAt(x, z)` is the short form for an untilted body. A point over no water answers `null`.

The surface the code reads is the full wave sum; a coarse far ring of an ocean draws less of it (see above), so near the horizon the two can
differ by the short waves, which are small.

## The Water shader

`builtin:water` also works on its own, on any mesh, which is how a flat plane of water was made before the component. With `u_waveSpace` at its
default (0) the waves are measured on the world's xz plane, nothing is lifted and the surface is taken to lie flat. The component turns it to 1 (the
mesh's uv is its local x and z) for the body's own space.

- **Caustics.** `u_causticStrength` (0 turns them off), `u_causticScale` (the size of one cell, in world units) and `u_causticSpeed` shape the web of light on what is under the water, seen from
  above and, through the effect, from below.
- **Waves.** `u_waveLength`, `u_waveHeight`, `u_waveSpeed`, `u_waveDirection`; small ripples (`u_rippleStrength`, `u_rippleLength`) ride on them and
  fade out with distance, so far water is calm rather than aliased.
- **Refraction and depth.** What is under the water is the picture of the opaque scene, shifted by the waves in proportion to how much water there
  is. The depth is **measured**: before the water is drawn the frame takes a copy of how far each pixel of the scene is from the eye, so the water at
  a pixel is the difference between that and the water's own distance. The light is absorbed along that column, red first (`u_absorption`,
  `u_density`), and the water gives back its own colour, from `u_shallowColor` to `u_deepColor` as it deepens.
- **Reflection.** The sky, the lights and the environment reflect off it with a Fresnel term (water is 1.333), so it is clear looking down and
  mirror-like at a grazing angle. Add Screen Space Reflections to the volume for reflections of the scene itself.
- **Foam.** Where the column is shallower than `u_foamWidth` (the shore, a rock, a pier's legs) and on the crests of the waves (`u_foamCrest`),
  broken up by moving noise.

## Limits

What is not there yet, so nobody is surprised:

- It needs the same engine-copied scene as glass (a post-process chain is running) and a device whose render targets can hold floats for the depth.
  Without the chain the water is drawn over the scene as a translucent surface; without float targets the depth is `u_fallbackDepth` everywhere, so
  there is no shore foam and no change of colour with depth.
- Blended surfaces (a pane, a particle, a leaf card) are ordered against water and glass by the pixel: when a frame has both, it is drawn in layers (the opaque scene, the blended draws
  over it for the picture the water refracts, the opaque scene put back and the water drawn, then the blended draws again depth-tested against the water). A blended thing under the surface is
  seen through it, one in front lies on it, and where there is no water it is blended once. It costs two more full-screen copies of the scene and a second pass over the blended draws, only in frames
  that have both. A blended draw with no depth test, or one that crosses the surface, is drawn whole on both sides of it, and one in front of the water is seen through it a second time, faintly,
  as the picture the water refracts holds it.
- A river does not carve the ground, rapids and falls are not foamed by how fast or steep the water runs, and two bodies do not blend where they meet.
  A lake's outline is a polygon on a plane: it does not follow a curved shore, and the waves die toward the shore without breaking on it.
- Things float (see [Floating](#floating)) and make a wake (see [Ripples](#ripples)); nothing swims yet.
- Caustics and shafts follow the first directional light only, from a pattern and not a simulation: the web does not respond to the actual shape of the waves. From above the caustics
  take what is under the water to face up (the picture holds no normals there), so a wall under the surface is lit as a floor would be.
- The mirror from below is screen-space: it shows only what the frame has on screen, and a ray that leaves the picture or meets nothing finds the water's own colour.
- The body of water a camera is under is the first the surface query finds at the camera's place: a camera under two overlapping bodies takes the numbers of the first.
- Ripples only bend the shading and carry foam: they do not lift the geometry, they are one window of a body following the camera (a body far from every camera keeps none), and a
  body keeps one window, so two cameras in a split screen share the first's. Foam that a ripple leaves is not carried by a river's current.
- The waves are the four peaked sines, not a spectrum, and they lift the surface without pushing it sideways.
