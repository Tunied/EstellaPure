Estella runs full-screen post-processing through ping-pong framebuffers. Add a
`PostProcessVolume` component, enable the effects you want, and tune their
parameters — in the editor or in code. Effects share the ping-pong buffers, so
several can stack in one frame at no extra target cost beyond the passes.

## Built-in effects

| Effect | What it does | Key uniforms |
|---|---|---|
| `bloom` | Glow on bright areas | `threshold`, `intensity`, `radius` |
| `tonemap` | ACES filmic tonemapping | `exposure` |
| `colorGrade` | Exposure / contrast / saturation / white balance | `exposure`, `contrast`, `saturation`, `temperature`, `tint` |
| `lutGrade` | Color grading through a lookup-table texture | `u_lut` (texture), `intensity` |
| `vignette` | Darkened screen edges | `intensity`, `softness` |
| `chromaticAberration` | RGB fringing | `intensity` |
| `blur` | Gaussian blur | `intensity` |
| `fxaa` | Fast anti-aliasing | `intensity` |
| `distanceFog` | Linear fog by distance from the camera | `near`, `far`, `intensity`, colour |
| `heightFog` | Exponential height fog with the sun's glow and a hazy horizon | `density`, `heightFalloff`, `baseHeight`, `sunAzimuth`, `sunElevation`, `skyFog`, colours |
| `lightShafts` | Sun rays through whatever stands in front of it | `intensity`, `sunAzimuth`, `sunElevation`, `decay`, `reach` |
| `autoExposure` | Eye adaptation: exposure follows how bright the picture is | `bias`, `speedUp`, `speedDown`, `minEV`, `maxEV` |

### Anti-aliasing before the chain

Geometry edges are smoothed by **MSAA on the scene itself**, not by an effect: 4×
by default, on WebGL2 and on WebGPU — which is what every native build (iOS,
Android, desktop, Steam) runs on. `createWebApp({ msaaSamples })` sizes it, and
`msaaSamples: 1` turns it off where the fill cost matters more than the edges.
`fxaa` is the cheaper, blurrier alternative for devices where even one sample
extra is too much.

### Rain, wet ground and puddles

`rain` is a storm in two parts. **Falling rain** is three layers of thin slanted streaks scrolling down the
screen, each standing at its own distance: a streak is hidden by a surface nearer than its layer and drawn
over one farther away, and the sky counts as infinitely far. **Wet ground** is found from the depth buffer —
a surface that faces up, flat enough to hold water — and darkened by `wetness`, because water in the pores takes
the light a dry surface scatters. Drops that land on it leave **ripples** (rings that spread and fade) and
**splashes** (the small quick crown of a drop on ground that is not water), laid out on a grid in the world so they
stay on the ground when the camera moves. Ripples need drops: with `intensity` at 0 the ground is wet and still.

**Puddles** are standing water: `puddles` is how much of the flat ground is under it and `puddleScale` how big the
pools are, a world length. A pool is darker still, takes denser and clearer ripples, and no splashes. Where a puddle
lies is a function of the world position alone, so it does not move with the camera. A puddle is also a *mirror*, and
that is the reflections effect's part: give `ssr` the same `puddles` and `puddleScale` and the pools the rain
darkens are the pools that reflect — flat, whatever the ground under them, and far more reflective than a dry surface,
with a sharp reflection where a dry one is blurred. Wet ground in general is shinier than dry: raise `ssr`'s `intensity`
when it rains.

The clock is the game's. A capture that has to be the same on every run sets `timeScale` to 0 and `phase` to the time it
wants. What this is not: it does not know a material's roughness or the real shape of the ground, so a puddle is
a patch of flat ground whatever it lies on, and the rain is a screen-space layer, not particles that collide with the world.

### Auto exposure

`autoExposure` is the eye: it measures how bright the picture is and settles the exposure that puts
it at middle grey. The measurement is the average of the **log** of the luminance, since brightness
is judged by ratio and a raw average is decided by whatever lamp is in view; pixels darker than 2^-8
are left out, because a black backdrop is not a dark scene. The result moves toward its target over time
from where the last frame left it — brighter faster (`speedUp`) than darker (`speedDown`), per second, so it does not
depend on the frame rate — and `minEV` / `maxEV` bound how far it may go. `bias` adds stops to the result for a
scene that should sit darker or brighter than grey. It works in the HDR chain; in an 8-bit chain the
measurement is coarse and the adaptation steps are visible. Put it before the output transform and
before `bloom`, so the glow sees the exposure the eye settled on.

### Depth of field

`depthOfField` blurs what is not at `focusDistance` (a world length from the camera), leaving a slab `focusRange` deep
sharp. The blur radius, in pixels, grows with how far a surface is from the focus as a share of its distance, up to `maxBlur`;
`aperture` is the radius of something infinitely far, and the sky counts as that. Each pixel gathers its neighbours over its own
circle, counting a neighbour only to the extent that neighbour's own blur reaches back: a sharp object in front of a
blurred background does not bleed into it, while a blurred foreground spreads over what is behind it. The gather is
rotated per pixel, so it leaves a fine grain that `taa` averages away.

### The G-buffer

Some effects have to know what a surface IS, not only how it looks. An effect whose shader says `#pragma gbuffer` makes the
frame draw a second colour attachment beside the scene's, in the same pass and from the same shaders: **octahedral normal in
`r` and `g`, roughness in `b`, metalness in `a`** (`a` is 0 where nothing wrote, and `0.75 + 0.25 × metalness`, so an edge that multisampling averaged with an unwritten pixel (at most 0.5) is told from a surface
where something did), 8 bits a channel. The effect finds it at `u_gbuffer` (GLSL) or `t6` (WGSL, read with `textureLoad`).

It costs one extra RGBA8 target and one extra write per pixel, and only while an enabled effect asks: a frame with no such
effect has no attachment and compiles no variant. What writes it today is the built-in **Model** shader — what an imported
glTF material becomes, with roughness, metalness and normal map as the material says — and the engine's plain lit mesh, which records
what it is: a rough dielectric. A sprite, a blended surface, an unlit mesh and a custom shader leave it at 0, and an effect must
treat that as "unknown" and leave the pixel alone, as `ssr` does.
A shader of your own can write it: it is compiled with `ES_GBUFFER` defined when the pass has the attachment, and then declares
a second output (`layout(location = 1) out vec4`, or a struct return in WGSL) and writes the same encoding.

On WebGL2 and WebGPU alike the attachment is multisampled with the scene and resolved with it. A blended draw never writes
it — a normal averaged with the one behind it is neither's — and the scene's own background clears it to 0.

### Fog and atmosphere

`heightFog` is fog that thins with height: a valley fills, a hilltop clears. Its density falls
off exponentially, so how much of it a ray crosses has a closed form — no marching — and the same
formula makes the **horizon hazy and the zenith clear**, because a ray skimming the ground stays in
the thick part and one aimed up leaves it at once. Sky pixels have no surface to measure to, so they
take the formula with a very long ray; `skyFog` says how much of that haze the sky keeps. Looking toward
the sun the haze takes `sunColor`: give it the sun's direction as `sunAzimuth` (degrees from +Z toward
+X) and `sunElevation` (degrees above the horizon) — these are settings of the effect, not read from
a `Light`, so keep them in step with the sun you placed.

`lightShafts` finds the sun's light through whatever stands in front of it, in screen space: each pixel
marches toward the sun's place on screen and counts how much of the way is open sky. It needs the
sun on screen or just off it, and only what the depth buffer holds can cast a shaft. Together the two
give the haze-and-rays look of a low sun; neither is a shadowed volume — fog does not know where a
shadow map says the light is blocked, so the fog itself is lit evenly.

Fog colours are authored as **sRGB** and converted for a linear project, the way a clear colour
and a light colour are, so the colour you pick is the colour a fully fogged surface shows.

### Screen-space reflections

`ssr` reflects what is on screen in the surfaces that face up: a wet street, a polished
floor, a lake's edge. The reflected ray is marched through the world
and projected back at every step, and a step that lands just behind what the depth buffer shows
is a hit. What it finds is blended over the pixel by a Schlick Fresnel term: faint face-on,
strong at a grazing angle, so the floor shines toward the horizon. A farther hit is blurred more,
which stands in for roughness.

What a surface is comes from [the G-buffer](#the-g-buffer): where the scene wrote one, the reflection uses the
surface's own normal (a normal map's detail, which depth cannot see), its roughness and its metalness. A rough surface
reflects dimly and blurred, and one rougher than `maxRoughness` is skipped before the ray is marched, which is most of the
cost; a metal reflects nearly all it receives where a dielectric reflects 4% face-on. `wetness` makes ground
smoother (a film of water fills the pores), so it reflects as it gets wet, and puddles are mirrors. Where nothing wrote
a record — a sprite, a blended or unlit surface, a custom shader — nothing is guessed: the pixel is left as it was. `floorBias`
(0 by default) can narrow what reflects further: at 1 only up-facing surfaces do.
Where the ray leaves the picture or goes on to the sky, the surface still reflects the **environment** the frame's meshes
reflect, at the sharpness its roughness gives; `skyReflection` says how much (0 turns it off). So a puddle shows the sky and
what lies beyond the edge of the picture, not only the props on screen. `maxDistance` is a
world length; the step count is fixed, so a longer reach is a coarser march, and the per-pixel
start offset that hides the banding is what `taa` averages away — use them together.

### Temporal anti-aliasing

`taa` smooths what MSAA cannot reach — shader aliasing and thin geometry — by
accumulating frames. While the effect is in the chain the engine shifts the picture by
a different sub-pixel amount each frame (eight positions, repeating), keeps the effect's
last output, and blends the two. Where a pixel was last frame comes from the depth
buffer: the pixel is un-projected to the world and projected with the previous
frame's camera, so a moving camera keeps its history. `blend` is the share of each
frame that is new: lower is smoother and slower to follow a change, 1 is no accumulation.

Put it **first** in the chain, so everything after it sees the settled image, and keep
`msaaSamples` on — the two add. Each camera keeps its own history. What it does not
know is how an object moves by itself: an object that moves is a different surface
from its history, which is clipped to the colours around it, so fast movers can look
slightly soft. It needs scene depth like SSAO does, so on WebGPU it cannot run with
multisampling on a scene that has no depth resolve.

The uniform names above are the friendly labels; **in code they are `u_`-prefixed**
(`u_intensity`, `u_exposure`, …). Most effects are identity at their defaults
(`colorGrade`, `blur`, `chromaticAberration`, `fxaa`, `lensDistortion`, `lutGrade`),
so adding them changes nothing until you dial them in. A few are visible the moment
you add them — `grayscale` (full desaturation), `vignette`, `bloom`, `outline`, and
`tonemap` (the ACES curve always applies) — so enable those only when you want them.

## HDR (linear color space)

When the project renders in the **linear color space** (see
[Lighting](/docs/graphics/lighting/)), the effect chain runs in **half-float HDR**
wherever float render targets are available — always on WebGPU, and on WebGL2 with
the near-universal `EXT_color_buffer_float`. Light accumulation past `1.0` (a
`Light` with `intensity` above 1) then *survives* into the chain instead of
clamping at the 8-bit store:

- **`bloom` with `threshold` above 1** blooms only over-range energy — bright
  lights glow while ordinary LDR art stays crisp. This is the classic "emissive
  glow" setup: crank a light's `intensity`, set `threshold` ≈ 1.2–1.5.
- **`tonemap`** receives true HDR input, so the ACES curve rolls off highlights
  instead of shaping already-clamped values.
- Without a tonemap pass, over-range values simply clip at the final
  linear→sRGB encode — fine for subtle glow, harsh for large over-exposure.

In gamma mode (or without float-target support) the chain is LDR and behaves as
before; a `threshold` above ~0.75 then never fires.

## Output transform

A scene lit in linear space can hold light brighter than a display can show. The
**output transform** is the one curve the finished frame goes through on its way to
the screen — **once, after every effect** — and it is a project setting, not an
effect: **Settings → Project → Rendering → Output transform**.

| Transform | What it does | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **None** | Encodes only. Values above 1 clip. | Flat 2D art, UI, anything that must round-trip exactly. |
| **ACES** | A filmic shoulder: highlights roll off, shadows deepen. It desaturates as it goes, so a very bright red drifts toward cream. | Lit 3D scenes that want a filmic look. |
| **Neutral** | Khronos PBR Neutral: the same roll-off, but hue and saturation survive. A sunlit red stays red. | Product shots, stylised art, anything whose colours are the point. |

It applies to the editor viewport, to Play and to every export, and changing it
needs no reload. In code: `outputTransform: 'none' | 'aces' | 'neutral'`.

<Aside>
The `tonemap` *effect* in the list above is a separate, optional ACES pass inside the
chain. With an output transform set you usually want neither it nor a second curve:
pick the output transform and leave the effect off.
</Aside>

## Using a `PostProcessVolume`

Add a `PostProcessVolume`, enable effects, and set their parameters. Volumes
**blend where they overlap**, so you can drive effects by area (a "underwater"
volume) or fade them in over a transition.

| Property | Type | Default | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| `effects` | list | `[]` | `{ type, enabled, uniforms }` per effect. |
| `isGlobal` | boolean | `true` | Apply everywhere vs. only inside the volume shape. |
| `shape` | `'box' \| 'sphere'` | `'box'` | Bounds shape for a local volume. |
| `size` | Vec2 | `{5, 5}` | Volume extents (local volumes). |
| `priority` | number | `0` | Higher-priority volumes win where they overlap. |
| `weight` | number | `1` | Blend weight, 0..1 (fade the whole volume). |
| `blendDistance` | number | `0` | Falloff distance at the volume edge. |

## Programmatic control

For a fixed camera pipeline in code, use the `PostProcess` resource. A stack starts
empty — add one **pass** per effect. Get the effect's shader from the registry with
`getEffectDef(type).factory()`, then set its `u_`-prefixed uniforms and bind the
stack to a camera:

```typescript
import { defineSystem, Query, Res, Camera, PostProcess, getEffectDef } from 'esengine';

const setup = defineSystem([Query(Camera), Res(PostProcess)], (q, pp) => {
  const grade = getEffectDef('colorGrade')!;
  for (const [camera] of q) {
    const stack = pp.createStack();
    stack.addPass('colorGrade', grade.factory())
         .setUniform('colorGrade', 'u_saturation', 1.3)
         .setUniform('colorGrade', 'u_contrast', 1.1);
    pp.bind(camera, stack);   // pp.unbind(camera) to remove
  }
});
```

A **multi-pass** effect like `bloom` exposes `def.multiPass`; add each sub-pass
(`for (const p of grade.multiPass!) stack.addPass(p.name, p.factory())`). For
anything beyond a fixed pipeline, a `PostProcessVolume` is simpler — it resolves
multi-pass and texture params for you.

| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| `PostProcess.createStack()` | Create an empty effect stack. |
| `stack.addPass(name, shader)` | Add an effect pass; `shader` from `getEffectDef(type).factory()` (chainable). |
| `stack.setUniform(pass, uniform, value)` | Set a `u_`-prefixed parameter (chainable). |
| `stack.setTexture(pass, uniform, handle)` | Bind a texture parameter, e.g. a LUT (chainable). |
| `stack.setEnabled(name, on)` | Toggle an already-added pass (chainable). |
| `PostProcess.bind(camera, stack)` / `unbind(camera)` | Attach / remove a camera's stack. |
| `getEffectDef(type)` / `getAllEffectDefs()` / `getEffectTypes()` | Look up effect definitions (shader factory + uniforms). |

### Texture parameters (LUTs & masks)

Some effects take a texture, not just scalars. `lutGrade` samples a **color
lookup table** — a 2D-packed `1024×32` PNG (32 slices of a 32³ cube) — so you can
author a whole grade in an image editor and drop it in. Add the pass, then bind the
texture with `setTexture` (or, on a `PostProcessVolume`, list it under the effect's
`textures: { u_lut: <ref> }`):

```typescript
stack.addPass('lutGrade', getEffectDef('lutGrade')!.factory())
     .setTexture('lutGrade', 'u_lut', lutHandle)
     .setUniform('lutGrade', 'u_intensity', 1);
```

## Best practices

- **Order matters** — passes run in the order you add them (or, on a volume, the
  order of its `effects[]`) and share the buffers, so a sensible chain is
  bloom → tonemap → color grade → vignette.
- **Use volumes for locality** (an area's mood) and the `PostProcess` API for a
  fixed camera pipeline.
- **Fade with `weight`** on a volume for smooth transitions instead of toggling.
- **Keep the stack short** on low-end/mobile — each pass is a full-screen draw.

<Aside type="note">
  Effects are pure-GLSL and data-driven from a registry, so the editor and runtime
  share one list — there's no hard-coded effect set to drift.
</Aside>
